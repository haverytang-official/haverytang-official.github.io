Vector analysis: from geometry to local and global field behavior

Leyan TANG · Classical Electrodynamics · Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics, Chapter 1, Sections 1.1–1.3

Vector analysis supplies the language used to describe electromagnetic fields. These notes connect vector geometry, local field variation, and the integrals that link a region to its boundary.

Study guide · Sections 1.1–1.3

Geometry → local variation → accumulated behavior

Three questions connect the material:

  1. What do dot and cross products tell us geometrically?
  2. How do gradient, divergence, and curl describe different kinds of local variation?
  3. How do local changes accumulate into endpoint differences, flux, and circulation?

Notation and scope. Bold symbols denote real three-dimensional vectors; A=|𝐀|A=|\mathbf A| denotes a magnitude. The unit vectors 𝐱^,𝐲^,𝐳^\hat{\mathbf x},\hat{\mathbf y},\hat{\mathbf z} form a fixed, right-handed orthonormal Cartesian basis. Scalar fields are written f,g,Tf,g,T, and vector fields 𝐀,𝐁,𝐯\mathbf A,\mathbf B,\mathbf v. Derivative formulas apply where the fields are sufficiently smooth; quotient formulas additionally require g≠0g\ne0.

1.1 Vector algebra

1.1.1 Vectors, scalars, and the elementary operations

A vector has magnitude and direction. A free vector can be translated without changing the vector itself; this does not mean that location is irrelevant in physics. A vector field assigns a vector to each position. A scalar has no spatial direction, but its value may be positive, negative, or zero and may carry units.

Addition is constructed head to tail. Subtraction adds the opposite vector. Multiplication by a negative scalar reverses direction:

𝐀+𝐁=𝐁+𝐀,(𝐀+𝐁)+𝐂=𝐀+(𝐁+𝐂).\mathbf A+\mathbf B=\mathbf B+\mathbf A,\qquad (\mathbf A+\mathbf B)+\mathbf C=\mathbf A+(\mathbf B+\mathbf C).
𝐀-𝐁=𝐀+(-𝐁),a(𝐀+𝐁)=a𝐀+a𝐁.\mathbf A-\mathbf B=\mathbf A+(-\mathbf B),\qquad a(\mathbf A+\mathbf B)=a\mathbf A+a\mathbf B.

1.1.2 Dot product: projection and angle

For nonzero vectors with angle θ\theta between them,

𝐀·𝐁=ABcosθ.\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=AB\cos\theta.

The result is a scalar: the magnitude of one vector multiplied by the signed projection of the other along it. It is positive for an acute angle, zero for perpendicular vectors, and negative for an obtuse angle. Parallel vectors pointing in opposite directions therefore have dot product -AB-AB, rather than ABAB.

𝐀·𝐁=𝐁·𝐀,𝐀·(𝐁+𝐂)=𝐀·𝐁+𝐀·𝐂.\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf A,\qquad \mathbf A\cdot(\mathbf B+\mathbf C)=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B+\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf C.

Since 𝐀·𝐀=A2\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A=A^2, the difference 𝐂=𝐀-𝐁\mathbf C=\mathbf A-\mathbf B gives a useful geometric check:

C2=(𝐀-𝐁)·(𝐀-𝐁)=A2+B2-2ABcosθ.C^2=(\mathbf A-\mathbf B)\cdot(\mathbf A-\mathbf B)=A^2+B^2-2AB\cos\theta.

1.1.3 Cross product: oriented area

For nonparallel vectors,

𝐀×𝐁=ABsinθ𝐧^.\mathbf A\times\mathbf B=AB\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}.

The unit normal 𝐧^\hat{\mathbf n} is chosen by the right-hand rule, curling from the first vector toward the second. The magnitude is the area of their parallelogram. Reversing the order reverses the normal:

𝐁×𝐀=-𝐀×𝐁,𝐀×(𝐁+𝐂)=𝐀×𝐁+𝐀×𝐂.\mathbf B\times\mathbf A=-\mathbf A\times\mathbf B,\qquad \mathbf A\times(\mathbf B+\mathbf C)=\mathbf A\times\mathbf B+\mathbf A\times\mathbf C.

Parallel vectors, including antiparallel ones, have zero cross product. The cross product of a vector with itself is the zero vector 𝟎\mathbf 0. When the cross product vanishes, its direction is undefined; the area interpretation still works.

1.1.4 Components and a worked angle calculation

Components are projections onto the basis vectors:

𝐀=Ax𝐱^+Ay𝐲^+Az𝐳^,Ax=𝐀·𝐱^.\mathbf A=A_x\hat{\mathbf x}+A_y\hat{\mathbf y}+A_z\hat{\mathbf z},\qquad A_x=\mathbf A\cdot\hat{\mathbf x}.

The other components are obtained in the same way. Addition and scalar multiplication act component by component. Orthonormality gives

𝐀·𝐁=AxBx+AyBy+AzBz,A=Ax2+Ay2+Az2.\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=A_xB_x+A_yB_y+A_zB_z,\qquad A=\sqrt{A_x^2+A_y^2+A_z^2}.

In the right-handed basis, 𝐱^×𝐲^=𝐳^\hat{\mathbf x}\times\hat{\mathbf y}=\hat{\mathbf z} and its cyclic versions hold; swapping the factors changes the sign. Thus

𝐀×𝐁=(AyBz-AzBy)𝐱^+(AzBx-AxBz)𝐲^+(AxBy-AyBx)𝐳^.\mathbf A\times\mathbf B=(A_yB_z-A_zB_y)\hat{\mathbf x}+(A_zB_x-A_xB_z)\hat{\mathbf y}+(A_xB_y-A_yB_x)\hat{\mathbf z}.

The determinant is a compact mnemonic for this expansion:

𝐀×𝐁=|𝐱^𝐲^𝐳^AxAyAzBxByBz|.\mathbf A\times\mathbf B=\begin{vmatrix}\hat{\mathbf x}&\hat{\mathbf y}&\hat{\mathbf z}\\A_x&A_y&A_z\\B_x&B_y&B_z\end{vmatrix}.

Worked example. For 𝐀=(1,0,1)\mathbf A=(1,0,1) and 𝐁=(0,1,1)\mathbf B=(0,1,1), the dot product is 11 and both magnitudes are 2\sqrt{2}. Therefore cosθ=1/2\cos\theta=1/2, giving θ=60°\theta=60^{\circ}.

1.1.5 Triple products: volume and the importance of brackets

The scalar triple product is unchanged by cyclic permutations:

𝐀·(𝐁×𝐂)=𝐁·(𝐂×𝐀)=𝐂·(𝐀×𝐁).\mathbf A\cdot(\mathbf B\times\mathbf C)=\mathbf B\cdot(\mathbf C\times\mathbf A)=\mathbf C\cdot(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B).

Its absolute value is the parallelepiped volume; its sign records orientation. Swapping any two vectors reverses the sign. In components,

𝐀·(𝐁×𝐂)=|AxAyAzBxByBzCxCyCz|.\mathbf A\cdot(\mathbf B\times\mathbf C)=\begin{vmatrix}A_x&A_y&A_z\\B_x&B_y&B_z\\C_x&C_y&C_z\end{vmatrix}.

For vector triple products, the BAC–CAB identity gives

𝐀×(𝐁×𝐂)=𝐁(𝐀·𝐂)-𝐂(𝐀·𝐁).\mathbf A\times(\mathbf B\times\mathbf C)=\mathbf B(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf C)-\mathbf C(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B).

Changing the brackets changes the expression:

(𝐀×𝐁)×𝐂=𝐁(𝐀·𝐂)-𝐀(𝐁·𝐂).(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf C=\mathbf B(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf C)-\mathbf A(\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf C).

Cross products are therefore not associative. The first result lies in the plane spanned by 𝐁,𝐂\mathbf B,\mathbf C; the second lies in the plane spanned by 𝐀,𝐁\mathbf A,\mathbf B.

Two further identities can be reduced using the same rule:

𝐀×[𝐁×(𝐂×𝐃)]=𝐁[𝐀·(𝐂×𝐃)]-(𝐀·𝐁)(𝐂×𝐃).\mathbf A\times[\mathbf B\times(\mathbf C\times\mathbf D)]=\mathbf B[\mathbf A\cdot(\mathbf C\times\mathbf D)]-(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B)(\mathbf C\times\mathbf D).
(𝐀×𝐁)·(𝐂×𝐃)=(𝐀·𝐂)(𝐁·𝐃)-(𝐀·𝐃)(𝐁·𝐂).(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\cdot(\mathbf C\times\mathbf D)=(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf C)(\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf D)-(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf D)(\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf C).

1.1.6 Source point and field point

In a field calculation, 𝐫′\mathbf r^{\prime} locates the source and 𝐫\mathbf r locates the point where the field is evaluated. Define the separation vector from source to field point as

𝐑=𝐫-𝐫′,R=|𝐫-𝐫′|,𝐑^=𝐫-𝐫′R.\mathbf R=\mathbf r-\mathbf r^{\prime},\qquad R=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r^{\prime}|,\qquad \hat{\mathbf R}=\frac{\mathbf r-\mathbf r^{\prime}}{R}.

This direction matters: exchanging the two points reverses 𝐑\mathbf R. The unit vector requires R≠0R\ne0. In later source integrals, derivatives with respect to 𝐫\mathbf r act while the source coordinate is held fixed.

1.2 Differential calculus

1.2.1 Gradient: from a total differential to steepest increase

For a differentiable scalar field T(x,y,z)T(x,y,z), a small displacement produces the first-order change described by

dT=∂T∂xdx+∂T∂ydy+∂T∂zdz=∇T·d𝐫.dT=\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}dx+\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}dy+\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}dz=\nabla T\cdot d\mathbf r.

Here d𝐫=dx𝐱^+dy𝐲^+dz𝐳^d\mathbf r=dx\hat{\mathbf x}+dy\hat{\mathbf y}+dz\hat{\mathbf z} and

∇T=∂T∂x𝐱^+∂T∂y𝐲^+∂T∂z𝐳^.\nabla T=\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\hat{\mathbf x}+\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\hat{\mathbf y}+\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\hat{\mathbf z}.

For a finite displacement, the corresponding relation is a local linear approximation, not an exact formula for an arbitrary distance. Along a unit direction 𝐮^\hat{\mathbf u}, with path distance ss,

dTds=∇T·𝐮^=|∇T|cosθ.\frac{dT}{ds}=\nabla T\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}=|\nabla T|\cos\theta.

If the gradient is nonzero, its direction gives the steepest increase per unit distance and its magnitude gives that maximum rate. A direction perpendicular to the gradient has zero first-order change. For a temperature field measured in kelvin and positions in metres, the gradient has units of kelvin per metre.

A landscape picture. If h(x,y)h(x,y) describes height, its gradient in the horizontal plane points in the direction of fastest uphill change. Along a contour of constant height, dh=0dh=0, so the gradient is perpendicular to the contour wherever it is nonzero. More generally, ∇T\nabla T is normal to a regular level surface T=constantT=\mathrm{constant}. Closely spaced contours, drawn at equal increments of the field, indicate a larger gradient magnitude.

Connection to physics. In electrostatics,

𝐄=-∇φ.\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi.

The electric field points toward decreasing electric potential, and is normal to regular equipotential surfaces. This scalar-potential formula alone does not describe a general electric field induced by a changing magnetic field. In a fluid or an MHD model with isotropic pressure pp, the pressure force per unit volume is -∇p-\nabla p: a pressure difference creates a force toward lower pressure.

Stationary does not necessarily mean extreme. At a differentiable interior point, ∇T=𝟎\nabla T=\mathbf 0 means all first-order directional changes vanish. It does not by itself establish a maximum or minimum: there can be a saddle or a stationary inflection. For example, T=x2-y2T=x^2-y^2 has zero gradient at the origin but increases along one axis and decreases along the other.

1.2.2 Del is an operator, not an ordinary vector

In Cartesian coordinates,

∇=𝐱^∂∂x+𝐲^∂∂y+𝐳^∂∂z.\nabla=\hat{\mathbf x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\hat{\mathbf y}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+\hat{\mathbf z}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}.

1.2.3 Divergence: local outflow, not just arrow size

∇·𝐯=∂vx∂x+∂vy∂y+∂vz∂z.\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}.

Divergence measures the limiting net outward flux through a small closed surface divided by its enclosed volume. Positive values indicate local source-like behavior and negative values local sink-like behavior. It is evaluated at each point; a positive value does not require a singular source located there.

For example, 𝐯=κ(x,y,z)\mathbf v=\kappa(x,y,z) with constant κ\kappa has divergence 3κ3\kappa everywhere. A large uniform vector field has zero divergence: its arrows are large, but their spatial derivatives vanish.

The small-ball test. Imagine a tiny fixed sphere in a flow. Net outward flow indicates positive divergence; net inward flow indicates negative divergence. Equal inflow and outflow give zero divergence, even though fluid may be moving through the sphere. The test compares flow across the entire small surface, not just the sizes of two arrows.

In SI electromagnetic notation, Gauss's law reads

∇·𝐄=ρε0,\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},

where ρ\rho is charge density and ε0\epsilon_0 is vacuum permittivity. It relates electric-field divergence to a physical source density.

For particles of a conserved species, with number density nn and mean velocity 𝐮\mathbf u, the source-free continuity equation is

∂n∂t+∇·(n𝐮)=0.\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(n\mathbf u)=0.

The vector n𝐮n\mathbf u is the particle flux density. Positive outward divergence means local density decreases with time. Conservation does not generally mean inflow equals outflow at every instant; that balance applies in a steady state without sources. Ionization, recombination, or other production and loss processes require source terms for the affected species.

1.2.4 Curl: local circulation and its axis

∇×𝐯=|𝐱^𝐲^𝐳^∂/∂x∂/∂y∂/∂zvxvyvz|.\nabla\times\mathbf v=\begin{vmatrix}\hat{\mathbf x}&\hat{\mathbf y}&\hat{\mathbf z}\\\partial/\partial x&\partial/\partial y&\partial/\partial z\\v_x&v_y&v_z\end{vmatrix}.

Expanding gives

∇×𝐯=(∂vz∂y-∂vy∂z)𝐱^+(∂vx∂z-∂vz∂x)𝐲^+(∂vy∂x-∂vx∂y)𝐳^.\nabla\times\mathbf v=\left(\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial z}\right)\hat{\mathbf x}+\left(\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial x}\right)\hat{\mathbf y}+\left(\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}\right)\hat{\mathbf z}.

Its component along a unit normal describes the limiting circulation per unit area around a small loop normal to that direction. The orientation follows the right-hand rule. “Local rotation” is a useful picture, but a curl value alone does not determine the shape of an entire field line.

As a check, the rigid-rotation velocity field 𝐯=Ω(-y,x,0)\mathbf v=\Omega(-y,x,0), with constant Ω\Omega, has ∇·𝐯=0\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0 and ∇×𝐯=2Ω𝐳^\nabla\times\mathbf v=2\Omega\hat{\mathbf z}. Divergence and curl describe different aspects of the same field.

The tiny-paddle-wheel picture. A small freely rotating paddle wheel can translate with a flow and also rotate about its own center. Curl concerns the local tendency to rotate, rather than translation. For a fluid velocity field, the local angular velocity associated with its antisymmetric velocity gradient is half the curl. A real floating leaf is only a qualitative analogy: its motion also depends on its size, shape, and interaction with the fluid.

For a two-dimensional flow 𝐮=(ux,uy,0)\mathbf u=(u_x,u_y,0) independent of zz, the relevant component is

(∇×𝐮)z=∂uy∂x-∂ux∂y.(\nabla\times\mathbf u)_z=\frac{\partial u_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u_x}{\partial y}.

This compares two transverse changes. Straight streamlines can still have nonzero curl: the shear flow 𝐮=(γy,0,0)\mathbf u=(\gamma y,0,0) has ∇×𝐮=-γ𝐳^\nabla\times\mathbf u=-\gamma\hat{\mathbf z} for constant γ\gamma. Conversely, the visual curvature of a streamline alone is not a test for curl.

In electromagnetism, the SI microscopic Ampère–Maxwell law is

∇×𝐁=μ0𝐉+μ0ε0∂𝐄∂t.\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.

Here 𝐉\mathbf J is total electric current density. The shorter relation ∇×𝐁≃μ0𝐉\nabla\times\mathbf B\simeq\mu_0\mathbf J applies when the displacement-current term is negligible; magnetostatics is one such case. Faraday's law instead relates electric-field curl to magnetic-field change:

∇×𝐄=-∂𝐁∂t.\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}.

These equations describe circulation of electromagnetic fields; they do not mean that an electric or magnetic field is literally a rotating fluid. MIT's discussion of the differential laws of Ampère and Faraday connects these forms to their integral statements.

1.2.5 Product rules: let each derivative act on each factor

Gradient, divergence, and curl are linear and commute with multiplication by a constant scalar. For products of varying fields, extra terms appear. The six useful product rules are

∇(fg)=f∇g+g∇f.\nabla(fg)=f\nabla g+g\nabla f.
∇(𝐀·𝐁)=(𝐀·∇)𝐁+(𝐁·∇)𝐀+𝐀×(∇×𝐁)+𝐁×(∇×𝐀).\nabla(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B)=(\mathbf A\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B+(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf A+\mathbf A\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B)+\mathbf B\times(\nabla\times\mathbf A).
∇·(f𝐀)=f(∇·𝐀)+𝐀·∇f.\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf A)=f(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)+\mathbf A\cdot\nabla f.
∇·(𝐀×𝐁)=𝐁·(∇×𝐀)-𝐀·(∇×𝐁).\nabla\cdot(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)=\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A)-\mathbf A\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B).
∇×(f𝐀)=f(∇×𝐀)+(∇f)×𝐀.\nabla\times(f\mathbf A)=f(\nabla\times\mathbf A)+(\nabla f)\times\mathbf A.
∇×(𝐀×𝐁)=(𝐁·∇)𝐀-(𝐀·∇)𝐁+𝐀(∇·𝐁)-𝐁(∇·𝐀).\nabla\times(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)=(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf A-(\mathbf A\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B+\mathbf A(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)-\mathbf B(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A).

The directional operator in these formulas means

(𝐀·∇)𝐁=Ax∂𝐁∂x+Ay∂𝐁∂y+Az∂𝐁∂z.(\mathbf A\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B=A_x\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial x}+A_y\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial y}+A_z\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial z}.

It returns a vector; the derivatives act on 𝐁\mathbf B here. A reliable derivation strategy is to expand one Cartesian component, apply the ordinary one-variable product rule, and regroup. For instance, expanding ∂(fAx)/∂x\partial(fA_x)/\partial x and adding the other two components gives the divergence product rule. MIT’s divergence product-rule discussion develops this approach.

1.2.6 Quotient rules and a sign check

Treat division by gg as multiplication by g-1g^{-1} and use ∇(g-1)=-∇g/g2\nabla(g^{-1})=-\nabla g/g^2. Where g≠0g\ne0,

∇(fg)=g∇f-f∇gg2.\nabla\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)=\frac{g\nabla f-f\nabla g}{g^2}.
∇·(𝐀g)=g(∇·𝐀)-𝐀·∇gg2.\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\mathbf A}{g}\right)=\frac{g(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)-\mathbf A\cdot\nabla g}{g^2}.
∇×(𝐀g)=g(∇×𝐀)-(∇g)×𝐀g2=g(∇×𝐀)+𝐀×∇gg2.\nabla\times\left(\frac{\mathbf A}{g}\right)=\frac{g(\nabla\times\mathbf A)-(\nabla g)\times\mathbf A}{g^2}=\frac{g(\nabla\times\mathbf A)+\mathbf A\times\nabla g}{g^2}.

The order of the cross product controls the sign. The correction term is negative when written as (∇g)×𝐀(\nabla g)\times\mathbf A and positive when written as 𝐀×∇g\mathbf A\times\nabla g. This follows directly from the curl product rule.

For a quick check, take 𝐀=𝐱^\mathbf A=\hat{\mathbf x} and g=yg=y in the region y≠0y\ne0. Direct differentiation gives ∇×(𝐱^/y)=𝐳^/y2\nabla\times(\hat{\mathbf x}/y)=\hat{\mathbf z}/y^2, agreeing with the plus sign in the last form.

1.2.7 Second derivatives and the Laplacian

Taking the divergence of a scalar field’s gradient defines its scalar Laplacian:

∇2T=∇·(∇T)=∂2T∂x2+∂2T∂y2+∂2T∂z2.\nabla^2T=\nabla\cdot(\nabla T)=\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial z^2}.

For fields with continuous second partial derivatives, mixed derivatives commute. Their cancellation gives two important identities:

∇×(∇T)=𝟎,∇·(∇×𝐯)=0.\nabla\times(\nabla T)=\mathbf 0,\qquad \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf v)=0.

For example, the zz component of the first identity is ∂2T/∂x∂y-∂2T/∂y∂x=0\partial^2T/\partial x\partial y-\partial^2T/\partial y\partial x=0. The zero in the curl identity is a vector; the zero in the divergence identity is a scalar. Singular points must be examined separately.

In a fixed Cartesian basis, the vector Laplacian acts on each component:

∇2𝐯=(∇2vx)𝐱^+(∇2vy)𝐲^+(∇2vz)𝐳^.\nabla^2\mathbf v=(\nabla^2v_x)\hat{\mathbf x}+(\nabla^2v_y)\hat{\mathbf y}+(\nabla^2v_z)\hat{\mathbf z}.

The curl-of-curl identity relates the remaining second-order operations:

∇×(∇×𝐯)=∇(∇·𝐯)-∇2𝐯.\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf v)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf v)-\nabla^2\mathbf v.

In general, ∇2𝐯\nabla^2\mathbf v is not the same as ∇(∇·𝐯)\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf v). Their difference is a curl-of-curl term. For example, 𝐯=(y2,0,0)\mathbf v=(y^2,0,0) has ∇(∇·𝐯)=𝟎\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf v)=\mathbf 0 but ∇2𝐯=2𝐱^\nabla^2\mathbf v=2\hat{\mathbf x}.

Coordinate caution. These compact component formulas use a fixed Cartesian basis. The scalar Laplacian also exists in cylindrical and spherical coordinates, but its component expression changes. The local unit vectors in those coordinates depend on position; applying the vector Laplacian requires the associated basis derivatives and coordinate factors. The geometric identities still hold in Euclidean space when the operators are expressed correctly.

Why this operator will recur. Combining the electrostatic relations 𝐄=-∇φ\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi and ∇·𝐄=ρ/ε0\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0 gives Poisson's equation:

∇2φ=-ρε0.\nabla^2\phi=-\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}.

In a charge-free electrostatic region this becomes Laplace's equation, ∇2φ=0\nabla^2\phi=0. For a smooth scalar field, the Laplacian measures the sum of spatial second derivatives; it is not the slope itself. It connects the potential's spatial curvature to charge density.

In vacuum with no charges or currents, electromagnetic fields satisfy a different equation:

∇2𝐄-1c2∂2𝐄∂t2=𝟎,c=1μ0ε0.\nabla^2\mathbf E-\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}=\mathbf 0,\qquad c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}.

The magnetic field obeys the corresponding wave equation. The spatial Laplacian is balanced by a time derivative: this describes propagation, rather than an electrostatic boundary-value problem. MIT's wave-equation discussion derives this connection from Maxwell's equations.

1.3.1 Line, surface, and volume integrals

Line integrals select a tangential component. For an oriented path CC from 𝐚\mathbf a to 𝐛\mathbf b,

∫C𝐯·d𝐥,d𝐥=𝐭^ds.\int_C\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf l,\qquad d\mathbf l=\hat{\mathbf t}\,ds.

Here dsds is a positive length element and 𝐭^\hat{\mathbf t} is the unit tangent in the direction of traversal. The dot product selects the field component along the path. Reversing the path reverses the sign. With a parameterized path 𝐫(t)\mathbf r(t), from t0t_0 to t1t_1,

∫C𝐯·d𝐥=∫t0t1𝐯[𝐫(t)]·d𝐫dtdt.\int_C\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf l=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\mathbf v[\mathbf r(t)]\cdot\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}\,dt.

For a force field, the work is W=∫C𝐅·d𝐥W=\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf l. In general it depends on the path. A conservative force has a path-independent work integral in its domain; a gradient field gives a direct example below. On a closed path, the notation ∮C𝐯·d𝐥\oint_C\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf l denotes circulation. Work has units of energy, whereas circulation has units of the particular field multiplied by length.

Surface integrals select a normal component. For an oriented surface SS,

∫S𝐯·d𝐚,d𝐚=𝐧^dS.\int_S\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf a,\qquad d\mathbf a=\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS.

The flux counts how much field crosses the surface in the chosen normal direction. Components tangent to the surface do not contribute to the dot product. Changing the normal reverses the sign. On a closed surface bounding a volume, the usual convention is the outward normal; the closed-surface flux is written with ∮\oint. An open surface has no automatic “outward” direction, so its orientation must be chosen.

The field's physical meaning determines the flux's units. For fluid velocity 𝐮\mathbf u, the surface integral gives volume flow per time. For mass flux density 𝐣m=ρm𝐮\mathbf j_m=\rho_m\mathbf u, where ρm\rho_m is mass density, it gives mass flow per time. An electric-field flux is a different physical quantity. A general surface flux depends on the surface; special independence results require additional conditions.

Volume integrals add contributions throughout a region. With dτ=dxdydzd\tau=dx\,dy\,dz in Cartesian coordinates,

∫VTdτ.\int_V T\,d\tau.

If TT is a density, this gives the corresponding total quantity. Vector integrals can be evaluated component by component in a fixed Cartesian basis:

∫V𝐯dτ=𝐱^∫Vvxdτ+𝐲^∫Vvydτ+𝐳^∫Vvzdτ.\int_V\mathbf v\,d\tau=\hat{\mathbf x}\int_V v_x\,d\tau+\hat{\mathbf y}\int_V v_y\,d\tau+\hat{\mathbf z}\int_V v_z\,d\tau.

These three geometries matter: a path has endpoint boundaries, a surface has boundary curves, and a volume has a closed surface as its boundary.

1.3.2 The fundamental theorem and the gradient theorem

For a continuously differentiable one-variable function,

∫abdfdxdx=f(b)-f(a).\int_a^b\frac{df}{dx}\,dx=f(b)-f(a).

Adding all the local changes yields the total change between endpoints. The integrand here is the derivative of ff, not ff itself. If an integral is written ∫abF(x)dx\int_a^b F(x)\,dx, we first seek a primitive ff with df/dx=Fdf/dx=F.

Along a path in a continuously differentiable, single-valued scalar field TT, the chain rule gives

dT[𝐫(t)]dt=∇T·d𝐫dt.\frac{dT[\mathbf r(t)]}{dt}=\nabla T\cdot\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}.

Integrating therefore gives the gradient theorem:

∫C∇T·d𝐥=T(𝐛)-T(𝐚),∮C∇T·d𝐥=0.\int_C\nabla T\cdot d\mathbf l=T(\mathbf b)-T(\mathbf a),\qquad \oint_C\nabla T\cdot d\mathbf l=0.

Walking up a hill along different routes changes the distance and effort, but not the elevation difference between the same two endpoints. The gradient integral measures that difference. For example, with dimensionless coordinates and T=x2+y2T=x^2+y^2, the integral from (0,0,0)(0,0,0) to (1,1,0)(1,1,0) is 22, whether the path is straight or follows the coordinate axes.

In electrostatics this becomes

∫𝐚𝐛𝐄·d𝐥=-[φ(𝐛)-φ(𝐚)].\int_{\mathbf a}^{\mathbf b}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-[\phi(\mathbf b)-\phi(\mathbf a)].

Path independence has a domain. If the field is already the gradient of a single-valued potential, this conclusion holds for paths in that potential's domain. Merely finding zero curl is a local test; to infer a global potential from it, a common sufficient setting is a simply connected region with a continuously differentiable field. Holes or excluded singularities need separate consideration.

1.3.3 The divergence theorem: sources and outward flux

Let VV be a bounded volume with a piecewise smooth closed boundary ∂V\partial V, oriented outward. If 𝐯\mathbf v has continuous first derivatives on an open region containing the volume and its boundary, then

∫V∇·𝐯dτ=∮∂V𝐯·d𝐚.\int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf v\,d\tau=\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf a.

The left side adds local net-outflow density throughout the volume; the right side measures total outward flux through its boundary. When the volume is divided into small cells, flux across a shared face leaves one cell and enters the next. Those internal contributions cancel. The surviving faces belong to the outer boundary. This is the divergence theorem, also called Gauss's theorem. OpenStax states its conditions and geometric interpretation.

A check on a sphere. For 𝐯=κ𝐫\mathbf v=\kappa\mathbf r, with constant κ\kappa, divergence is 3κ3\kappa. On a sphere of radius aa centered at the origin, the outward normal component is κa\kappa a. The two sides agree:

3κ(4πa33)=(κa)(4πa2)=4πκa3.3\kappa\left(\frac{4\pi a^3}{3}\right)=(\kappa a)(4\pi a^2)=4\pi\kappa a^3.

Using Gauss's law for a smooth charge distribution gives

∮∂V𝐄·d𝐚=1ε0∫Vρdτ=Qencε0.\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a=\frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho\,d\tau=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\epsilon_0}.

For a fixed volume and a source-free conserved particle species, the continuity equation gives

ddt∫Vndτ=-∮∂Vn𝐮·d𝐚.\frac{d}{dt}\int_V n\,d\tau=-\oint_{\partial V}n\mathbf u\cdot d\mathbf a.

This reads directly as a balance: particle content decreases by the net outward particle flow. It retains the storage term that is lost if we simply say “inflow equals outflow.” Point-charge singularities require an appropriate limiting or distributional treatment when applying the electric-field law.

1.3.4 Stokes' theorem: local circulation and boundary circulation

Let SS be an oriented, piecewise smooth surface with boundary curve ∂S\partial S, and let 𝐯\mathbf v have continuous first derivatives in an open region containing the surface. Then

∫S(∇×𝐯)·d𝐚=∮∂S𝐯·d𝐥.\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf v)\cdot d\mathbf a=\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf l.

The normal and the boundary direction are linked by the right-hand rule: the thumb points along the chosen normal, and the curled fingers indicate positive traversal. Viewed from the tip of the normal toward the surface, that traversal is counterclockwise. Reversing the normal requires reversing the boundary direction too. OpenStax gives the theorem and its orientation convention.

Subdivide the surface into small consistently oriented patches. Their boundary circulations run in opposite directions on every shared edge. Adding them cancels internal edges and leaves only the outer boundary. This explains how local circulation accumulates into total boundary circulation.

A circular check. For 𝐯=Ω(-y,x,0)\mathbf v=\Omega(-y,x,0) and an upward-oriented disk of radius aa, the curl is 2Ω𝐳^2\Omega\hat{\mathbf z}. The counterclockwise boundary speed is Ωa\Omega a, so

∫S(∇×𝐯)·d𝐚=2Ωπa2=(Ωa)(2πa)=∮∂S𝐯·d𝐥.\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf v)\cdot d\mathbf a=2\Omega\pi a^2=(\Omega a)(2\pi a)=\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf l.

What is independent of the spanning surface? For two admissible surfaces with the same oriented boundary, the flux of the curl of the same smooth field is identical, because each equals the same boundary circulation. This does not make every surface flux independent of the surface. Another useful case is a divergence-free field: its fluxes through two such surfaces agree when those surfaces bound a volume in which the field remains smooth and divergence-free.

A closed surface has no boundary curve. For a field smooth on a neighborhood of that surface,

∮S(∇×𝐯)·d𝐚=0.\oint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf v)\cdot d\mathbf a=0.

This follows from the absence of a boundary, rather than from shrinking a boundary to one point. In the xyxy-plane, the circulation form of Green's theorem is a planar special case of Stokes' theorem.

For a fixed loop and a fixed spanning surface, Faraday's law yields

∮∂S𝐄·d𝐥=-ddt∫S𝐁·d𝐚.\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_S\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a.

A changing magnetic flux gives a circulating electric field. Holding the geometry fixed matters: a moving circuit also involves the motional contribution to electromotive force.

1.3.5 Integration by parts: keep the boundary term

The ordinary product rule can be integrated:

d(fg)dx=fdgdx+gdfdx.\frac{d(fg)}{dx}=f\frac{dg}{dx}+g\frac{df}{dx}.

Combining this with the fundamental theorem gives

∫abfdgdxdx=[fg]ab-∫abgdfdxdx.\int_a^b f\frac{dg}{dx}\,dx=[fg]_a^b-\int_a^b g\frac{df}{dx}\,dx.

Integration by parts transfers a derivative from one factor to the other, at the cost of an endpoint term. The same strategy works for a scalar field ff and a vector field 𝐀\mathbf A. Start with

∇·(f𝐀)=f∇·𝐀+𝐀·∇f.\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf A)=f\nabla\cdot\mathbf A+\mathbf A\cdot\nabla f.

Integrate over VV, then apply the divergence theorem:

∫Vf∇·𝐀dτ=∮∂Vf𝐀·d𝐚-∫V𝐀·∇fdτ.\int_V f\nabla\cdot\mathbf A\,d\tau=\oint_{\partial V}f\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf a-\int_V\mathbf A\cdot\nabla f\,d\tau.

The surface term records what happens at the boundary. It can vanish under suitable conditions, for example if f=0f=0 on the boundary or 𝐀·𝐧^=0\mathbf A\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}=0 there. Decay at infinity must be strong enough to make the limiting surface integral vanish. A boundary term should be checked, rather than removed automatically.

1.1.1 矢量、标量与基本运算

矢量具有大小和方向。自由矢量可以平移而不改变矢量本身，但这并不意味着位置在物理中不重要：矢量场会给每一个位置分配一个矢量。标量没有空间方向，但其数值可以为正、负或零，也可以带有单位。

矢量加法采用首尾相接的作图方式；减法相当于加上反向矢量。乘以负标量会反转方向：

𝐀+𝐁=𝐁+𝐀,(𝐀+𝐁)+𝐂=𝐀+(𝐁+𝐂).\mathbf A+\mathbf B=\mathbf B+\mathbf A,\qquad (\mathbf A+\mathbf B)+\mathbf C=\mathbf A+(\mathbf B+\mathbf C).

1.1.2 点积：投影与夹角

对于夹角为 θ\theta 的非零矢量，

𝐀·𝐁=ABcosθ.\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=AB\cos\theta.

结果是标量：一个矢量的大小，乘以另一个矢量沿其方向的带符号投影。夹角为锐角时结果为正，垂直时为零，钝角时为负。因此，平行但方向相反的矢量点积为 -AB-AB，而不是 ABAB。

𝐀·𝐁=𝐁·𝐀,𝐀·(𝐁+𝐂)=𝐀·𝐁+𝐀·𝐂.\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf A,\qquad \mathbf A\cdot(\mathbf B+\mathbf C)=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B+\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf C.

利用 𝐀·𝐀=A2\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A=A^2，令 𝐂=𝐀-𝐁\mathbf C=\mathbf A-\mathbf B，可以得到一个几何检查：

1.1.3 叉积：带方向的面积

对于不平行的矢量，

𝐀×𝐁=ABsinθ𝐧^.\mathbf A\times\mathbf B=AB\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}.

单位法向量 𝐧^\hat{\mathbf n} 由右手定则确定：手指从第一个矢量弯向第二个矢量。叉积的大小等于两矢量张成的平行四边形面积。交换顺序会反转法向：

𝐁×𝐀=-𝐀×𝐁,𝐀×(𝐁+𝐂)=𝐀×𝐁+𝐀×𝐂.\mathbf B\times\mathbf A=-\mathbf A\times\mathbf B,\qquad \mathbf A\times(\mathbf B+\mathbf C)=\mathbf A\times\mathbf B+\mathbf A\times\mathbf C.

平行矢量，包括方向相反的平行矢量，叉积均为零。一个矢量与自身的叉积为零矢量 𝟎\mathbf 0。当叉积为零时，它的方向没有定义，但面积的解释仍然成立。

1.2.3 散度：局部流出，而非箭头大小

∇·𝐯=∂vx∂x+∂vy∂y+∂vz∂z.\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}.

散度表示：一个小闭合面的净向外通量除以所围体积，在体积趋于零时的极限。正值表示局部类似源的行为，负值表示局部类似汇的行为。它在每一点都有定义；散度为正并不要求那个位置存在一个奇异的点源。

例如，𝐯=κ(x,y,z)\mathbf v=\kappa(x,y,z) 中 κ\kappa 为常量，则散度在各处均为 3κ3\kappa。一个很大的均匀矢量场却具有零散度：箭头虽大，但空间导数为零。

用小球检验。 想象在流场中放置一个很小、位置固定的球面。净流出对应正散度，净流入对应负散度；流入和流出相等则散度为零，即使流体仍不断穿过球面。这个检验比较的是整个小闭合面上的流量，不只是两个箭头的大小。

在 SI 制的电磁学记号下，高斯定律为

∇·𝐄=ρε0,\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},

其中 ρ\rho 是电荷密度，ε0\epsilon_0 是真空介电常数。它把电场的散度与实际的源密度联系起来。

对于粒子数守恒的一种粒子，设数密度为 nn、平均速度为 𝐮\mathbf u，无源连续性方程为

∂n∂t+∇·(n𝐮)=0.\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(n\mathbf u)=0.

矢量 n𝐮n\mathbf u 是粒子通量密度。净向外散度为正，意味着当地数密度随时间降低。守恒一般不意味着每一时刻流入都等于流出；无源稳态才满足这种平衡。若某种粒子存在电离、复合或其他产生与损失过程，其方程中还需要相应的源项。

1.2.4 旋度：局部环流及其轴向

∇×𝐯=|𝐱^𝐲^𝐳^∂/∂x∂/∂y∂/∂zvxvyvz|.\nabla\times\mathbf v=\begin{vmatrix}\hat{\mathbf x}&\hat{\mathbf y}&\hat{\mathbf z}\\\partial/\partial x&\partial/\partial y&\partial/\partial z\\v_x&v_y&v_z\end{vmatrix}.

展开后得到

∇×𝐯=(∂vz∂y-∂vy∂z)𝐱^+(∂vx∂z-∂vz∂x)𝐲^+(∂vy∂x-∂vx∂y)𝐳^.\nabla\times\mathbf v=\left(\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial z}\right)\hat{\mathbf x}+\left(\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial x}\right)\hat{\mathbf y}+\left(\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}\right)\hat{\mathbf z}.

旋度沿某单位法向的分量，描述与该法向垂直的小环路上，单位面积环流的极限。方向由右手定则决定。“局部旋转”是有用的物理图像，但仅靠某点的旋度，不能确定整条场线的形状。

作为检查，刚体转动速度场 𝐯=Ω(-y,x,0)\mathbf v=\Omega(-y,x,0) 中 Ω\Omega 为常量，则 ∇·𝐯=0\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0、∇×𝐯=2Ω𝐳^\nabla\times\mathbf v=2\Omega\hat{\mathbf z}。散度和旋度描述同一个场的不同方面。

用微小叶轮理解。 一个可以自由转动的小叶轮，既可能随流体平移，也可能绕自身中心转动。旋度描述局部转动趋势，不是平移速度。对于流体速度场，速度梯度的反对称部分所对应的局部角速度，是旋度的一半。漂浮树叶只能作为定性类比：真实运动还取决于大小、形状及其与流体的相互作用。

若二维流场 𝐮=(ux,uy,0)\mathbf u=(u_x,u_y,0) 不依赖 zz，相关分量为

(∇×𝐮)z=∂uy∂x-∂ux∂y.(\nabla\times\mathbf u)_z=\frac{\partial u_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u_x}{\partial y}.

它比较两个横向变化。流线是直线，也可能有非零旋度：剪切流 𝐮=(γy,0,0)\mathbf u=(\gamma y,0,0) 在 γ\gamma 为常量时，满足 ∇×𝐮=-γ𝐳^\nabla\times\mathbf u=-\gamma\hat{\mathbf z}。反过来，只看流线是否弯曲，也不能判断旋度。

在电磁学中，SI 制的微观安培–麦克斯韦定律为

∇×𝐁=μ0𝐉+μ0ε0∂𝐄∂t.\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.

其中 𝐉\mathbf J 是总电流密度。当位移电流项可以忽略时，才可采用简式 ∇×𝐁≃μ0𝐉\nabla\times\mathbf B\simeq\mu_0\mathbf J；静磁场是其中一种情形。法拉第定律则把电场旋度与磁场变化联系起来：

∇×𝐄=-∂𝐁∂t.\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}.

这些方程描述电磁场的环流，并不意味着电场或磁场像流体一样实际旋转。MIT 对安培与法拉第微分定律的讨论 将这些形式与相应的积分表达联系起来。

1.2.5 乘积法则：让导数作用到每个因子

梯度、散度和旋度均为线性运算，可以提出常量标量因子。但对于随位置变化的因子，会出现额外项。六条常用乘积法则为

∇(fg)=f∇g+g∇f.\nabla(fg)=f\nabla g+g\nabla f.
∇(𝐀·𝐁)=(𝐀·∇)𝐁+(𝐁·∇)𝐀+𝐀×(∇×𝐁)+𝐁×(∇×𝐀).\nabla(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B)=(\mathbf A\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B+(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf A+\mathbf A\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B)+\mathbf B\times(\nabla\times\mathbf A).
∇·(f𝐀)=f(∇·𝐀)+𝐀·∇f.\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf A)=f(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)+\mathbf A\cdot\nabla f.
∇·(𝐀×𝐁)=𝐁·(∇×𝐀)-𝐀·(∇×𝐁).\nabla\cdot(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)=\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A)-\mathbf A\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B).
∇×(f𝐀)=f(∇×𝐀)+(∇f)×𝐀.\nabla\times(f\mathbf A)=f(\nabla\times\mathbf A)+(\nabla f)\times\mathbf A.
∇×(𝐀×𝐁)=(𝐁·∇)𝐀-(𝐀·∇)𝐁+𝐀(∇·𝐁)-𝐁(∇·𝐀).\nabla\times(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)=(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf A-(\mathbf A\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B+\mathbf A(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)-\mathbf B(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A).

这些公式中的方向微分算符表示

(𝐀·∇)𝐁=Ax∂𝐁∂x+Ay∂𝐁∂y+Az∂𝐁∂z.(\mathbf A\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B=A_x\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial x}+A_y\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial y}+A_z\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial z}.

其结果是矢量，右侧的导数在这里作用于 𝐁\mathbf B。可靠的推导方法，是展开一个笛卡尔分量，应用普通的一元乘积法则，再将结果重组。例如，展开 ∂(fAx)/∂x\partial(fA_x)/\partial x，再加上另外两个分量，便得到散度乘积法则。MIT 的散度乘积法则说明 展示了这一方法。

将除以 gg 看成乘以 g-1g^{-1}，并使用 ∇(g-1)=-∇g/g2\nabla(g^{-1})=-\nabla g/g^2。在 g≠0g\ne0 的区域，有

1.2.7 二阶导数与拉普拉斯算符

对标量场的梯度取散度，定义其标量拉普拉斯算符：

∇2T=∇·(∇T)=∂2T∂x2+∂2T∂y2+∂2T∂z2.\nabla^2T=\nabla\cdot(\nabla T)=\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial z^2}.

若场的二阶偏导连续，则混合偏导可以交换次序。相应项相消，得到两个重要恒等式：

∇×(∇T)=𝟎,∇·(∇×𝐯)=0.\nabla\times(\nabla T)=\mathbf 0,\qquad \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf v)=0.

例如，第一个恒等式的 zz 分量为 ∂2T/∂x∂y-∂2T/∂y∂x=0\partial^2T/\partial x\partial y-\partial^2T/\partial y\partial x=0。旋度式中的零是矢量，散度式中的零是标量。奇异点需要单独分析。

在固定笛卡尔基底中，矢量拉普拉斯逐分量作用：

∇2𝐯=(∇2vx)𝐱^+(∇2vy)𝐲^+(∇2vz)𝐳^.\nabla^2\mathbf v=(\nabla^2v_x)\hat{\mathbf x}+(\nabla^2v_y)\hat{\mathbf y}+(\nabla^2v_z)\hat{\mathbf z}.

旋度的旋度将其余二阶运算联系起来：

∇×(∇×𝐯)=∇(∇·𝐯)-∇2𝐯.\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf v)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf v)-\nabla^2\mathbf v.

一般来说，∇2𝐯\nabla^2\mathbf v 不等于 ∇(∇·𝐯)\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf v)，两者之差涉及旋度的旋度。例如，𝐯=(y2,0,0)\mathbf v=(y^2,0,0) 有 ∇(∇·𝐯)=𝟎\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf v)=\mathbf 0，但 ∇2𝐯=2𝐱^\nabla^2\mathbf v=2\hat{\mathbf x}。

坐标提醒。 上述简洁分量式采用固定笛卡尔基底。标量拉普拉斯在柱坐标和球坐标中同样存在，但表达式会变化。那些坐标中的局部单位矢量依赖位置，计算矢量拉普拉斯时必须计入相应的基矢导数和坐标因子。在欧几里得空间中，只要正确表示各算符，几何恒等式仍然成立。

为什么它会反复出现。 将静电关系 𝐄=-∇φ\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi 与 ∇·𝐄=ρ/ε0\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0 结合，得到泊松方程：

∇2φ=-ρε0.\nabla^2\phi=-\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}.

在不含电荷的静电区域，它成为拉普拉斯方程 ∇2φ=0\nabla^2\phi=0。对于光滑标量场，拉普拉斯算符描述各空间方向的二阶导数之和，而不是斜率本身。它把电势的空间曲率与电荷密度联系起来。

在没有电荷和电流的真空中，电磁场满足另一种方程：

∇2𝐄-1c2∂2𝐄∂t2=𝟎,c=1μ0ε0.\nabla^2\mathbf E-\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}=\mathbf 0,\qquad c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}.

磁场满足相应的波动方程。这里空间拉普拉斯与时间导数相平衡，描述传播，而非静电边值问题。MIT 的波动方程说明 展示了它与麦克斯韦方程的联系。

1.3.1 线积分、面积分与体积分

线积分选取切向分量。 对于从 𝐚\mathbf a 到 𝐛\mathbf b 的有向路径 CC，

∫C𝐯·d𝐥,d𝐥=𝐭^ds.\int_C\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf l,\qquad d\mathbf l=\hat{\mathbf t}\,ds.

其中 dsds 是正的长度元，𝐭^\hat{\mathbf t} 是沿行进方向的单位切向量。点积选出场沿路径的分量，反转路径会反转积分的符号。若路径用 𝐫(t)\mathbf r(t) 参数化，参数从 t0t_0 变化到 t1t_1，则

∫C𝐯·d𝐥=∫t0t1𝐯[𝐫(t)]·d𝐫dtdt.\int_C\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf l=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\mathbf v[\mathbf r(t)]\cdot\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}\,dt.

对于力场，功为 W=∫C𝐅·d𝐥W=\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf l，一般会依赖路径。保守力在其定义域内的功积分与路径无关；下文的梯度场给出直接例子。闭合路径上的 ∮C𝐯·d𝐥\oint_C\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf l 表示环流。功的单位是能量，而一般环流的单位取决于被积场，等于该场的单位乘以长度。

面积分选取法向分量。 对于有向曲面 SS，

∫S𝐯·d𝐚,d𝐚=𝐧^dS.\int_S\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf a,\qquad d\mathbf a=\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS.

通量描述场沿选定法向穿过曲面的程度，切向分量不贡献点积。改变法向会反转符号。对于包围体积的闭合面，通常约定法向向外，并用 ∮\oint 表示闭合面通量。开放曲面没有自动确定的“向外”方向，需要先选择取向。

场的物理含义决定通量的单位。若被积场是流速 𝐮\mathbf u，面积分给出单位时间的体积流量；若是质量通量密度 𝐣m=ρm𝐮\mathbf j_m=\rho_m\mathbf u，其中 ρm\rho_m 为质量密度，则给出单位时间的质量流量。电场通量又是另一种物理量。一般面积分会依赖所选曲面，特殊的曲面无关性需要额外条件。

体积分累加整个区域的贡献。 在笛卡尔坐标中，dτ=dxdydzd\tau=dx\,dy\,dz，于是

∫VTdτ.\int_V T\,d\tau.

如果 TT 表示某种密度，积分便给出相应的总量。在固定笛卡尔基底下，矢量积分可以逐分量计算：

∫V𝐯dτ=𝐱^∫Vvxdτ+𝐲^∫Vvydτ+𝐳^∫Vvzdτ.\int_V\mathbf v\,d\tau=\hat{\mathbf x}\int_V v_x\,d\tau+\hat{\mathbf y}\int_V v_y\,d\tau+\hat{\mathbf z}\int_V v_z\,d\tau.

这三种几何对象的边界不同：路径的边界是端点，曲面的边界是曲线，体积的边界则是闭合面。

1.3.2 微积分基本定理与梯度定理

对于连续可微的一元函数，

∫abdfdxdx=f(b)-f(a).\int_a^b\frac{df}{dx}\,dx=f(b)-f(a).

把所有局部变化相加，就得到两端点之间的总变化。这里的被积函数是 ff 的导数，而不是 ff 本身。若积分写成 ∫abF(x)dx\int_a^b F(x)\,dx，应先寻找满足 df/dx=Fdf/dx=F 的原函数 ff。

沿连续可微、单值的标量场 TT 中的一条路径，链式法则给出

dT[𝐫(t)]dt=∇T·d𝐫dt.\frac{dT[\mathbf r(t)]}{dt}=\nabla T\cdot\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}.

积分后得到梯度定理：

∫C∇T·d𝐥=T(𝐛)-T(𝐚),∮C∇T·d𝐥=0.\int_C\nabla T\cdot d\mathbf l=T(\mathbf b)-T(\mathbf a),\qquad \oint_C\nabla T\cdot d\mathbf l=0.

沿不同路线上山，距离和付出的体力可以不同，但相同起终点的高度差不变。梯度的路径积分测量的正是这种差值。例如，在无量纲坐标下取 T=x2+y2T=x^2+y^2，从 (0,0,0)(0,0,0) 到 (1,1,0)(1,1,0) 的积分为 22，无论沿直线还是先后沿坐标轴行进。

在静电学中，这成为

∫𝐚𝐛𝐄·d𝐥=-[φ(𝐛)-φ(𝐚)].\int_{\mathbf a}^{\mathbf b}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-[\phi(\mathbf b)-\phi(\mathbf a)].

路径无关性有适用区域。 若场已经写成单值势函数的梯度，结论适用于该势函数定义域内的路径。仅检查旋度为零，是局部判断；若要据此推出全局势函数，一个常用的充分条件是场连续可微且区域单连通。区域中的孔洞或被排除的奇异点需要单独考虑。

1.3.3 散度定理：源与向外通量

设 VV 是有界体积，分段光滑的闭合边界 ∂V\partial V 取向向外。若 𝐯\mathbf v 在包含该体积及其边界的开放区域中具有连续一阶导数，则

∫V∇·𝐯dτ=∮∂V𝐯·d𝐚.\int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf v\,d\tau=\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf a.

左侧在体积内累加局部净流出密度，右侧测量整个边界上的净向外通量。将体积分成小单元后，穿过共享面的通量离开一个单元，同时进入另一个单元。这些内部贡献相消，剩下的面就是外边界。它称为散度定理，也称高斯定理。OpenStax 给出了适用条件和几何解释。

用球面检查。 对 𝐯=κ𝐫\mathbf v=\kappa\mathbf r，其中 κ\kappa 为常量，散度为 3κ3\kappa。在以原点为中心、半径为 aa 的球面上，向外法向分量为 κa\kappa a。两侧一致：

3κ(4πa33)=(κa)(4πa2)=4πκa3.3\kappa\left(\frac{4\pi a^3}{3}\right)=(\kappa a)(4\pi a^2)=4\pi\kappa a^3.

对光滑电荷分布使用高斯定律，得到

∮∂V𝐄·d𝐚=1ε0∫Vρdτ=Qencε0.\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a=\frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho\,d\tau=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\epsilon_0}.

对于固定体积和无源、粒子数守恒的一种粒子，连续性方程给出

ddt∫Vndτ=-∮∂Vn𝐮·d𝐚.\frac{d}{dt}\int_V n\,d\tau=-\oint_{\partial V}n\mathbf u\cdot d\mathbf a.

这可以直接读成收支平衡：体积内的粒子数减少量，等于净向外的粒子流量。它保留了简单说“流入等于流出”时被遗漏的储存项。对含点电荷奇异性的电场应用定律时，需要适当的极限或分布意义处理。

1.3.4 Stokes 定理：局部环流与边界环流

设 SS 是有向、分段光滑的曲面，边界为 ∂S\partial S；𝐯\mathbf v 在包含该曲面的开放区域中具有连续一阶导数。则

∫S(∇×𝐯)·d𝐚=∮∂S𝐯·d𝐥.\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf v)\cdot d\mathbf a=\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf l.

曲面法向与边界方向由右手定则联系起来：拇指指向选定法向，弯曲的四指指示边界正向。从法向箭头的尖端朝曲面看，正向是逆时针。若反转法向，也必须反转边界方向。OpenStax 给出了定理及其方向约定。

把曲面细分成取向一致的小块，每一条共享边在相邻两块的边界环流中，行进方向都相反。求和后内部边相消，只留下外边界。这解释了局部环流如何累积成整个边界的环流。

用圆形路径检查。 对 𝐯=Ω(-y,x,0)\mathbf v=\Omega(-y,x,0) 和半径为 aa、法向向上的圆盘，旋度为 2Ω𝐳^2\Omega\hat{\mathbf z}。边界上逆时针方向的速度为 Ωa\Omega a，因此

∫S(∇×𝐯)·d𝐚=2Ωπa2=(Ωa)(2πa)=∮∂S𝐯·d𝐥.\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf v)\cdot d\mathbf a=2\Omega\pi a^2=(\Omega a)(2\pi a)=\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf l.

究竟是什么与跨接曲面无关？ 对于具有相同有向边界的两个合适曲面，同一个光滑场的旋度的通量相同，因为两者都等于同一个边界环流。这并不意味着所有面积分都与曲面无关。另一种有用情形是无散场：若两个这样的曲面围成一个体积，且场在其中始终光滑、散度为零，则它们的通量也相同。

闭合曲面没有边界曲线。对于在该曲面邻域中光滑的场，

∮S(∇×𝐯)·d𝐚=0.\oint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf v)\cdot d\mathbf a=0.

原因是没有边界，而不是把边界缩成一个点。在 xyxy 平面上，格林定理的环流形式是 Stokes 定理的平面特例。

对于固定环路和固定跨接曲面，法拉第定律给出

∮∂S𝐄·d𝐥=-ddt∫S𝐁·d𝐚.\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_S\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a.

磁通量的变化产生环流电场。几何位置保持固定是这里的重要条件：运动回路的电动势还涉及运动贡献。

1.3.5 分部积分：保留边界项

普通乘积法则可以积分：

d(fg)dx=fdgdx+gdfdx.\frac{d(fg)}{dx}=f\frac{dg}{dx}+g\frac{df}{dx}.

结合微积分基本定理，得到

∫abfdgdxdx=[fg]ab-∫abgdfdxdx.\int_a^b f\frac{dg}{dx}\,dx=[fg]_a^b-\int_a^b g\frac{df}{dx}\,dx.

分部积分把导数从一个因子转移到另一个因子，代价是出现端点项。对标量场 ff 和矢量场 𝐀\mathbf A 也可以采用同样的思路。先写

∇·(f𝐀)=f∇·𝐀+𝐀·∇f.\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf A)=f\nabla\cdot\mathbf A+\mathbf A\cdot\nabla f.

在 VV 中积分，再用散度定理：

∫Vf∇·𝐀dτ=∮∂Vf𝐀·d𝐚-∫V𝐀·∇fdτ.\int_V f\nabla\cdot\mathbf A\,d\tau=\oint_{\partial V}f\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf a-\int_V\mathbf A\cdot\nabla f\,d\tau.

曲面项记录边界上的行为。只有满足适当条件，它才会消失，例如边界上 f=0f=0 或 𝐀·𝐧^=0\mathbf A\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}=0。若边界取到无穷远，场的衰减必须足以使极限面积分趋于零。边界项需要检验，不能自动删去。

复习检查

  1. 用投影解释：为什么反向平行矢量的点积为负，而叉积为零？
  2. 用分量展开叉积，检查中间分量的符号。
  3. 解释为什么驻点不一定是极值点。
  4. 对比均匀场、径向膨胀和刚体转动的散度与旋度。
  5. 从乘积法则推导旋度商法则，明确保留叉积顺序。
  6. 用旋度的旋度恒等式，区分矢量拉普拉斯与散度的梯度。
  7. 区分路径切向、曲面法向和体积元，并说明为什么开放曲面没有自动确定的“向外”方向。
  8. 解释梯度定理、散度定理和 Stokes 定理如何把局部导数与边界信息连接起来。
  9. 说明路径无关或跨接曲面无关成立时需要什么条件。
  10. 推导矢量分部积分公式，判断什么条件下边界项才能消失。

我的理解。 矢量乘积记录投影、面积与带方向的体积。微分算符描述局部行为，积分定理描述这些行为累积后的结果：斜率累积成端点差，源密度累积成净向外通量，局部环流累积成边界上的总环流。这是对同一个场的两种相互对应的观察方式，也说明了为什么物理定律可以同时具有微分形式和积分形式。使用时，需要追踪对哪个场求导、方向如何约定、采用什么坐标，以及假设在哪个区域成立。
