What Does a Magnetic Probe Actually Measure?

Reading Notes on Strait et al. (2008) · II.B.2 Magnetic field probes
By Leyan TANG

This reading note focuses on inductive magnetic probes. Five questions guide the discussion, tracing the measurement process from changing magnetic flux to information about the magnetic field.

Paper: E. J. Strait, E. D. Fredrickson, J.-M. Moret, and M. Takechi, Chapter 2: Magnetic Diagnostics, Fusion Science and Technology 53(2), 304–334 (2008). Original paper.

A physical picture to start with

An inductive magnetic probe can be pictured as a small coil placed at a particular location and oriented in a particular direction. When the magnetic flux through it changes, an electromotive force is induced; the measurement system records the corresponding voltage. One small coil provides local information about one field component. Multiple probes distributed across different positions form an array. Coils with different axes can be combined to measure several components near the same location.

The basic measurement chain is:

Changing magnetic field → changing flux through the coil → induced voltage → calibration and integration where needed → magnetic-field information.

The probe should be small compared with the spatial scale over which the field changes appreciably. The field can then be treated as approximately uniform across the coil, allowing the signal to be interpreted as a local measurement. Locality and the probe’s disturbance of the field are separate issues: small size alone does not guarantee that a probe leaves the field undisturbed.

Notation used throughout this note

Consider a stationary coil with NN turns and mean area AA per turn. Let n^c\hat{\mathbf n}_c denote its axial unit vector, normal to the plane of the coil.

Define the field component along the coil axis as:

Baxis=B·n^c.B_{\mathrm{axis}}=\mathbf B\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}_c.

The flux through one turn is φ=∫AB·n^cdA\phi=\int_A\mathbf B\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}_c\,\mathrm dA. The flux linkage, denoted here by Ψ\Psi, is the sum of the fluxes through all turns:

Ψ=∑i=1N∫AiB·n^idA≃NABaxis.\Psi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{A_i}\mathbf B\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}_i\,\mathrm dA \simeq NA B_{\mathrm{axis}}.

The final approximation assumes approximately aligned turn normals and a field that is nearly uniform across the coil. The ideal inductive response is:

V=-dΨdt≃-NAdBaxisdt.V=-\frac{\mathrm d\Psi}{\mathrm dt} \simeq-NA\frac{\mathrm dB_{\mathrm{axis}}}{\mathrm dt}.

The minus sign expresses Lenz’s law. The sign of the recorded voltage also depends on winding and wiring conventions. A real measurement chain includes the frequency response of the coil, cables, and acquisition electronics; the ideal relation is used within its applicable frequency range, with appropriate calibration.

“Axial” here means along the coil axis. In the discussion of shear perturbations below, “parallel” and “perpendicular” refer instead to the equilibrium magnetic field. These are different reference directions.

① Why does an unintegrated voltage signal emphasize magnetic fluctuations?

Question while reading: The equilibrium field is usually much larger than the fluctuation. Why is the unintegrated probe signal still useful for studying fluctuations?

My understanding: The probe responds to the rate of change, rather than to the field magnitude itself. For a given coil-axis component, write:

Baxis(t)=Beq,axis(t)+δBaxis(t).B_{\mathrm{axis}}(t)=B_{\mathrm{eq,axis}}(t)+\delta B_{\mathrm{axis}}(t).

If the equilibrium background changes slowly over a fluctuation period, then dBeq,axis/dt≃0\mathrm dB_{\mathrm{eq,axis}}/\mathrm dt\simeq0 is a useful approximation. A large background field can therefore contribute little induced voltage on that timescale.

For example, take a sinusoidal fluctuation:

δBaxis(t)=B~cos(ωt),ω=2πf.\delta B_{\mathrm{axis}}(t)=\widetilde B\cos(\omega t), \qquad \omega=2\pi f.

Its voltage response is:

Vδ(t)=NAωB~sin(ωt),|V~δ|=NAωB~.V_\delta(t)=NA\omega\widetilde B\sin(\omega t), \qquad |\widetilde V_\delta|=NA\omega\widetilde B.

For the same magnetic fluctuation amplitude, faster variation produces a larger ideal voltage amplitude. This is an amplitude relation. The voltage and magnetic fluctuation also have a phase relationship, so the instantaneous voltage cannot simply be written as ωNAB(t)\omega NA B(t).

A strictly static component produces no induced voltage. A slowly varying equilibrium component still produces a response. “Suppressing static or slowly varying background contributions” is therefore more accurate than “eliminating the equilibrium field entirely.” The usable high-frequency response is also limited by the diagnostic bandwidth.

Resolved: a large background field does not necessarily have a large time derivative.

② Why are probes often placed inside a conducting wall and oriented to measure a wall-tangential component?

Question while reading: Why does the vessel wall weaken a signal? What does the inside location achieve, and what does the tangential orientation achieve?

My understanding: Changing magnetic flux drives eddy currents in a conducting wall. Their magnetic field opposes the original flux change, so fluctuations measured outside the wall can be attenuated and phase delayed. Placing a probe on the plasma side of the relevant conducting structure reduces the shielding associated with the signal passing through that structure.

The choice of a tangential component concerns the direction of the perturbation near the boundary. Relative to the wall, decompose the field as:

B=Bnn^w+Bt,\mathbf B=B_n\hat{\mathbf n}_w+\mathbf B_t,

where n^w\hat{\mathbf n}_w is the wall normal and Bt\mathbf B_t lies in the wall’s tangent plane. For rapid variations in a regime where the wall approximates an ideal conductor, changes in the flux through it are strongly constrained. The corresponding normal perturbation can therefore be suppressed, while the tangential perturbation on the inner side is often a useful measurement quantity.

This does not mean that metal shields only the normal component, or that a tangential signal is unaffected. The actual response depends on frequency, conductivity, thickness, geometry, and other nearby conducting structures. Being “inside” also does not necessarily mean that no conductor lies between the plasma and the probe. Probe placement and the target frequency range need to be considered together. ITER magnetic-diagnostic studies discuss wall-current phase delays in external measurements and the influence of nearby structures on high-frequency probe response.

Resolved: position determines which conducting structures affect the signal; orientation determines which field component the coil selects.

③ Why focus on shear perturbations, and how does this relate to poloidal probes?

Question while reading: What distinguishes shear polarization from compressional polarization? Why do common probes measure in the poloidal direction tangent to the wall?

My understanding: Let B0\mathbf B_0 be the equilibrium field and δB\delta\mathbf B a small perturbation. Decompose the perturbation into components parallel and perpendicular to B0\mathbf B_0:

δB=δB∥+δB⊥.\delta\mathbf B=\delta\mathbf B_\parallel+\delta\mathbf B_\perp.
  • Shear-like perturbations: characterized primarily by δB⊥\delta\mathbf B_\perp. They can be pictured as bending field lines and changing the field direction.
  • Compressional perturbations: include a parallel component, δB∥\delta\mathbf B_\parallel, that changes the field strength.

For |δB|≪|B0||\delta\mathbf B|\ll|\mathbf B_0|, the first-order change in field strength is:

δ|B|≃b^0·δB,b^0=B0|B0|.\delta|\mathbf B|\simeq\hat{\mathbf b}_0\cdot\delta\mathbf B, \qquad \hat{\mathbf b}_0=\frac{\mathbf B_0}{|\mathbf B_0|}.

A perturbation strictly perpendicular to the equilibrium field therefore changes the direction at first order; its effect on field strength appears at higher order. “Shear” and “compressional” describe polarization characteristics. Real MHD modes can contain a mixture of components.

The equilibrium field in a tokamak can be written as:

B0=Bφφ^+Bp,\mathbf B_0=B_\varphi\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}+\mathbf B_p,

where BφB_\varphi is the toroidal component and Bp\mathbf B_p is the poloidal component. When the toroidal field dominates, a perturbation perpendicular to B0\mathbf B_0 lies approximately in the poloidal plane. Combined with the preference for a wall-tangential measurement near the boundary, this helps explain the common choice of a probe aligned with the poloidal tangent to the wall.

However, “perpendicular to the toroidal direction” specifies a plane, not a unique coil orientation. Wall geometry and the target perturbation are also needed. Nor should all MHD modes be treated as purely shear-like.

Resolved: shear polarization, a dominant toroidal field, and wall boundary conditions together help explain common poloidal-tangential measurements.

④ Why does a magnetic probe output voltage rather than magnetic field directly?

Question while reading: The magnetic field is the quantity of interest. Why does the instrument provide an induced voltage instead?

My understanding: This follows from the operating principle of an inductive coil. The field, coil area, and orientation determine the magnetic flux. A changing flux linkage induces an electromotive force according to Faraday’s law. The measurement system records the corresponding voltage, from which field information is inferred through calibration and signal processing.

“Magnetic probe” describes the measurement purpose; it does not mean that the coil directly outputs a reading in tesla. This discussion concerns inductive magnetic probes. The same derivative response should not be assumed for every magnetic-field sensor operating on a different principle.

For constant NN, AA, and coil orientation, ideal integration gives:

Baxis(t)-Baxis(t0)=-1NA∫t0tV(τ)dτ.B_{\mathrm{axis}}(t)-B_{\mathrm{axis}}(t_0) =-\frac{1}{NA}\int_{t_0}^{t}V(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau.

Integration recovers the field change relative to t0t_0. The voltage alone cannot determine an unknown, strictly static offset; an initial field value or independent reference is required. Calibration and baseline correction are also necessary, because even a small voltage offset accumulates into drift after integration.

Processed field and flux measurements can constrain equilibrium reconstruction. Reconstruction additionally requires measurements at multiple locations and a physical model; integrating one coil signal does not recover the equilibrium of the entire plasma. The ITER magnetic-diagnostic scheme describes the roles of integrated signals, external coil currents, and models in equilibrium reconstruction.

Resolved: voltage is the signal directly supplied by the sensor; magnetic field is inferred using geometry, calibration, and integration where needed.

⑤ What do effective area and number of turns mean?

Question while reading: Is NANA the physical area occupied by the probe? Why does increasing the number of turns increase the signal?

My understanding: The same time-varying field can thread multiple turns. The induced electromotive forces contributed by the turns add along the winding. For approximately coaxial turns with the same orientation and area, define:

Aeff=NA.A_{\mathrm{eff}}=NA.
  • NN: the number of turns, dimensionless.
  • AA: the mean area per turn, in m2\mathrm{m}^2.
  • AeffA_{\mathrm{eff}}: the area–turn product in this ideal model, also in m2\mathrm{m}^2. It is not the probe’s physical footprint.

For example, a coaxial coil with 100 turns, each of area 1cm21\,\mathrm{cm}^2, has NA=100cm2=0.01m2NA=100\,\mathrm{cm}^2=0.01\,\mathrm{m}^2. This does not mean that the coil occupies 100cm2100\,\mathrm{cm}^2 of space. It means that the induction from the same field change accumulates over multiple turns.

If the turn areas differ but their normals are approximately aligned, then Aeff=∑iAiA_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_i A_i, which can still be written as NANA using the mean area. If the orientations or field distribution also differ, the flux integral should be used instead of a single geometric area.

Adding turns changes the ideal sensitivity, but it also changes circuit properties such as inductance. The response of a real probe must be calibrated; more turns alone do not establish better measurement performance.

Resolved: NANA describes the accumulated induction from the turns, rather than a simple enlargement of the coil’s physical area.

磁探针究竟测量什么？

Strait 等（2008）· II.B.2 Magnetic field probes 阅读笔记
作者：Leyan TANG

这篇阅读笔记聚焦感应式磁探针，围绕五个阅读问题，梳理从磁通变化到磁场信息的测量过程。

阅读文献：E. J. Strait, E. D. Fredrickson, J.-M. Moret, M. Takechi, Chapter 2: Magnetic Diagnostics, Fusion Science and Technology 53(2), 304–334 (2008)。原文 DOI。

先建立一个直观图像

感应式磁探针可以理解为放在特定位置、朝向特定方向的小线圈。穿过线圈的磁通发生变化时，线圈产生感应电动势，测量系统把它记录为电压信号。一个小线圈提供一个方向的局部信息；在不同位置布置多个探针，才构成探针阵列。若要测量同一点附近的多个磁场分量，可以使用不同轴向的线圈组合。

因此，一条基本的测量链是：

磁场变化 → 穿过线圈的磁通变化 → 感应电压 → 校准与必要的积分 → 磁场信息。

探针的尺寸需要小于磁场显著变化的空间尺度，才能近似把线圈范围内的磁场视为均匀。这是把信号解释为“局部测量”的依据。测量的局部性与探针对磁场的扰动，是两个需要分别考虑的问题；尺寸小本身并不保证探针不会扰动磁场。

一组贯穿全文的符号

设线圈固定不动，具有 NN 匝，每匝平均面积为 AA；线圈轴向单位矢量为 n^c\hat{\mathbf n}_c，也就是线圈平面的法线方向。

定义轴向磁场分量：

Baxis=B·n^c.B_{\mathrm{axis}}=\mathbf B\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}_c.

单匝磁通为 φ=∫AB·n^cdA\phi=\int_A\mathbf B\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}_c\,\mathrm dA。多匝线圈的磁通链（flux linkage）是各匝磁通之和，本文用 Ψ\Psi 表示：

Ψ=∑i=1N∫AiB·n^idA≃NABaxis.\Psi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{A_i}\mathbf B\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}_i\,\mathrm dA \simeq NA B_{\mathrm{axis}}.

最后一步采用各匝近似同轴、线圈内磁场近似均匀的条件。理想感应式探针满足：

V=-dΨdt≃-NAdBaxisdt.V=-\frac{\mathrm d\Psi}{\mathrm dt} \simeq-NA\frac{\mathrm dB_{\mathrm{axis}}}{\mathrm dt}.

负号体现楞次定律；实际记录电压的正负还取决于绕向和接线约定。真实测量链也包含线圈、电缆和采集电路的频率响应，上式是经适当校准后、在适用频段内使用的理想关系。

这里的“轴向”是相对于线圈轴。后面讨论剪切扰动时的“平行或垂直”，则是相对于平衡磁场。两套方向不能混用。

① 为什么未积分电压能突出磁场涨落？

阅读时的疑问： 平衡磁场通常比涨落大得多，为什么未积分的探针信号仍适合研究涨落？

现在的理解： 探针响应的是变化率，而不是磁场大小本身。将同一个线圈轴向的分量写成：

Baxis(t)=Beq,axis(t)+δBaxis(t).B_{\mathrm{axis}}(t)=B_{\mathrm{eq,axis}}(t)+\delta B_{\mathrm{axis}}(t).

如果在一个涨落周期内，平衡背景变化很慢，就可以近似认为 dBeq,axis/dt≃0\mathrm dB_{\mathrm{eq,axis}}/\mathrm dt\simeq0。即使背景场很大，它对这个时间尺度上的感应电压贡献也可以很小。

例如令涨落为：

δBaxis(t)=B~cos(ωt),ω=2πf.\delta B_{\mathrm{axis}}(t)=\widetilde B\cos(\omega t), \qquad \omega=2\pi f.

则电压响应为：

Vδ(t)=NAωB~sin(ωt),|V~δ|=NAωB~.V_\delta(t)=NA\omega\widetilde B\sin(\omega t), \qquad |\widetilde V_\delta|=NA\omega\widetilde B.

在相同磁场涨落幅度下，变化越快，理想感应电压幅度越大。这一关系描述的是幅度响应。电压与磁场涨落之间还有相位关系，不能直接把瞬时电压写成 ωNAB(t)\omega NA B(t)。

严格静态的分量不产生感应电压；缓慢变化的平衡分量仍然会有响应。因此，“抑制静态或慢变背景”比“完全消除平衡磁场”更准确。实际高频测量还受诊断系统带宽限制。

已解决：背景场很大，并不代表它的时间导数也很大。

② 为什么常把探针放在导电壁内侧，并测量壁面的切向分量？

阅读时的疑问： 真空室壁为什么会削弱信号？“内侧”和“切向”各自解决什么问题？

现在的理解： 变化的磁通会在导电壁中驱动涡流（eddy currents）。涡流产生的磁场反抗原来的磁通变化，因此壁外测到的涨落可能被衰减，并出现相位延迟。把探针放在相关导电结构的等离子体一侧，可以减少信号穿过该结构时受到的屏蔽。

“测量切向分量”则涉及边界附近磁扰动的方向。相对于壁面，可将磁场分解为：

B=Bnn^w+Bt,\mathbf B=B_n\hat{\mathbf n}_w+\mathbf B_t,

其中 n^w\hat{\mathbf n}_w 是壁面法线，Bt\mathbf B_t 位于壁面切平面内。在快速变化、导电壁可近似视为理想导体的情况下，穿过壁面的磁通变化受到强烈约束，相关法向扰动因此可能被压低；壁内侧的切向扰动则常是有用的测量量。

不能由此认为“金属只屏蔽法向磁场”或“切向信号完全不受影响”。真实响应取决于频率、电导率、厚度、几何及附近其他导电结构；“内侧”也不一定意味着探针前面没有任何导电材料。应将安装位置与目标频段一起考虑。ITER 磁诊断研究也讨论了壁电流对壁外测量产生的相位延迟，以及附近结构对高频探针响应的影响。

已解决：位置决定信号经过哪些导电结构，朝向决定线圈选取哪个磁场分量。

③ 为什么关注剪切扰动？它与极向探针有什么关系？

阅读时的疑问： 剪切极化（shear polarization）与压缩极化（compressional polarization）有什么区别？为什么常见探针选择沿壁面的极向方向？

现在的理解： 设平衡磁场为 B0\mathbf B_0，小扰动为 δB\delta\mathbf B。将扰动分为相对于 B0\mathbf B_0 的平行分量与垂直分量：

δB=δB∥+δB⊥.\delta\mathbf B=\delta\mathbf B_\parallel+\delta\mathbf B_\perp.
  • 剪切型（shear-like）扰动： 以 δB⊥\delta\mathbf B_\perp 为主要特征，直观上更像弯曲磁力线、改变磁场方向。
  • 压缩型（compressional）扰动： 包含改变磁场强度的平行分量 δB∥\delta\mathbf B_\parallel。

当 |δB|≪|B0||\delta\mathbf B|\ll|\mathbf B_0| 时，磁场强度的一阶变化为：

δ|B|≃b^0·δB,b^0=B0|B0|.\delta|\mathbf B|\simeq\hat{\mathbf b}_0\cdot\delta\mathbf B, \qquad \hat{\mathbf b}_0=\frac{\mathbf B_0}{|\mathbf B_0|}.

所以，纯粹垂直于平衡磁场的扰动，在一阶近似下主要改变方向；它对磁场强度的改变出现在更高阶。这里的“剪切”和“压缩”描述扰动的极化特征，真实 MHD 模式可以混合不同分量。

托卡马克的平衡磁场可写为：

B0=Bφφ^+Bp,\mathbf B_0=B_\varphi\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}+\mathbf B_p,

其中 BφB_\varphi 为环向分量，Bp\mathbf B_p 为极向分量。在环向场占主导的情形下，垂直于 B0\mathbf B_0 的扰动大致位于极向平面。结合壁面附近的切向测量要求，沿壁面极向切线的探针便是常见选择。

但“垂直于环向方向”只限定一个平面，不能单独确定线圈朝向；还需要壁面几何和目标扰动信息。也不能把所有 MHD 模式都概括为纯剪切型。

已解决：剪切极化、环向场占主导和壁面边界条件，共同帮助理解常见的极向切向测量。

④ 为什么磁探针输出电压，而不是直接输出磁场？

阅读时的疑问： 磁场本身是想测量的量，为什么仪器给出的却是感应电压？

现在的理解： 这由感应式线圈的工作机制决定。磁场通过线圈的面积和朝向形成磁通，磁通链随时间变化，依据法拉第定律产生感应电动势。测量系统记录相应电压，再结合校准与信号处理推断磁场。

“磁探针”这个名称指它的测量目的，而不是说线圈直接输出以特斯拉为单位的读数。这里讨论的是感应式磁探针；不能把同样的变化率响应推广到采用其他原理的全部磁场传感器。

若 NN、AA 与线圈朝向不变，理想积分关系为：

Baxis(t)-Baxis(t0)=-1NA∫t0tV(τ)dτ.B_{\mathrm{axis}}(t)-B_{\mathrm{axis}}(t_0) =-\frac{1}{NA}\int_{t_0}^{t}V(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau.

积分恢复的是相对于 t0t_0 的磁场变化。仅凭电压信号，无法得到完全静态的未知常量；还需要初始磁场或独立参考。实际处理也需要校准和基线修正，否则微小电压偏置会在积分后积累成漂移。

处理后的磁场与磁通数据可为平衡重建提供约束，重建还需要多位置测量和物理模型，而不是只积分一只线圈就得到整个等离子体的平衡。ITER 磁诊断方案说明了积分信号、外部线圈电流及模型在平衡重建中的作用。

已解决：电压是传感器直接提供的信号，磁场是结合几何、校准和必要积分得到的物理量。

⑤ 有效面积与匝数分别代表什么？

阅读时的疑问： NANA 是探针实际占据的面积吗？为什么增加匝数会增加信号？

现在的理解： 同一个时变磁场可以穿过多匝线圈，每一匝贡献的感应电动势沿绕组叠加。对于近似同轴、同向、每匝面积相同的线圈，定义：

Aeff=NA.A_{\mathrm{eff}}=NA.
  • NN：匝数，无量纲。
  • AA：每匝平均面积，单位为 m2\mathrm{m}^2。
  • AeffA_{\mathrm{eff}}：本文理想模型中的面积—匝数乘积，单位仍为 m2\mathrm{m}^2，并不是探针的几何占地面积。

例如，100 匝、每匝面积 1cm21\,\mathrm{cm}^2 的同轴线圈，其 NA=100cm2=0.01m2NA=100\,\mathrm{cm}^2=0.01\,\mathrm{m}^2。这不意味着它占据了 100cm2100\,\mathrm{cm}^2 的空间，而是同一磁场变化被多匝累积感应。

如果各匝面积不同但法线近似相同，则 Aeff=∑iAiA_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_i A_i，并可用每匝平均面积写成 NANA。若朝向或磁场分布也不同，就应回到磁通积分，不能直接套用一个几何面积。

增加匝数会改变理想灵敏度，也会改变线圈的电感等电路性质。实际探针仍要通过校准确定响应，不能只凭 NN 越大就判断测量效果越好。

已解决：NANA 表示各匝感应贡献的累积，而不是把线圈物理面积简单放大。
